# [English Summary] Effects of Salt Level and Fat Ratio on AGEs and N-Nitrosamines in Salami

> A summary prepared for a 10-minute presentation. Each number is followed by its location in the paper. Values read by eye from graphs are marked "approx." Check them against the original before use.

## Bibliographic information

- **Title:** Formation of advanced glycation end-products and N-nitrosamines in salami of different recipes and fermented at different stages
- **Authors:** Wenjing Wang, Zixiong Song (co-first authors), Ying Jing, Xiaoxiao Wei, Haizhen Li, Jianhua Xie, Mingyue Shen (corresponding author)
- **Affiliation:** State Key Laboratory of Food Science and Resources, Nanchang University, China
- **Journal:** *Food Chemistry* 474 (2025) 143228. doi:10.1016/j.foodchem.2025.143228
- **Dates:** Received 5 Dec 2024, accepted 4 Feb 2025, available online 6 Feb 2025
- **License:** © 2025 Elsevier, all rights reserved (not open access)

## One-sentence summary

Even in salami, which is fermented and ripened at low temperature, AGEs (CML and CEL) and N-nitrosamines increased throughout ripening. Salt reduced CEL and some nitrosamines, while fat increased CML but reduced CEL and most nitrosamines. Within the AGEs, CML was linked more closely to lipid oxidation and CEL to protein-related Maillard reactions.

## 1. Background (about 1 min)

**Research gap**
- Most work on AGEs and N-nitrosamines (NAs) in meat has focused on **high-temperature processing**.
- Salami is not heated, but it is fermented and ripened for weeks and contains every reactant needed: protein, fat, free amino acids and nitrite (p.1).
- The effects of **salt level and lean-to-fat ratio** on AGE and NA formation in salami had not been studied (p.2).

**AGEs (advanced glycation end-products)**
- Formed by non-enzymatic reactions between sugars and proteins (Maillard reaction). The main markers are Nε-carboxymethyllysine (CML) and Nε-carboxyethyllysine (CEL) (p.1–2).
- Binding to the receptor RAGE increases reactive oxygen species and pro-inflammatory cytokines. AGE intake is associated with diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease and cancer (p.1).
- The precursors, α-dicarbonyls (3-DG, GO, MGO), are about 2000 times more reactive than glucose (p.9, citing Degen et al., 2012).

**N-nitrosamines (NAs)**
- Formed when biogenic amines or polyamines react with nitrite. During fermentation, proteins break down into peptides and amino acids; decarboxylation produces biogenic amines, which react with nitrite to form NAs (p.2).
- About 300 NAs have been identified, and some, such as NDMA and NDEA, are highly carcinogenic (p.2).

**Prior studies (as cited in the paper)**
- Minced beef with high fat contained more CML and CEL (Sun et al., 2017).
- About 73% of NAs in roasted lamb were in the fat portion (Yurchenko & Mölder, 2007).
- Salt at 0.5% inhibited AGE formation, while 1.5–5% promoted it (Niu et al., 2018).
- Adding 2% salt to cooked sausage reduced CML, NDMA and NDEA (Lu et al., 2023).

## 2. Objective (p.2)

To track how salt level (0, 2, 4%) and lean-to-fat ratio (10:0, 8:2, 6:4) affect AGE and NA formation in salami over 23 days, and to relate them to proximate composition, precursors and intermediates (α-dicarbonyls), as a basis for reducing these compounds.

## 3. Materials and methods (about 1 min)

### Sample preparation (Section 2.2)
| | Salt experiment | Fat experiment |
|---|---|---|
| Variable | NaCl 0 · 2 · 4% (w/w) | Lean:fat 10:0 · 8:2 · 6:4 (0 · 20 · 40% fat) |
| Fixed | Lean:fat 8:2 | NaCl 2% |
| Common | Glucose 1%, sodium nitrite 0.015%, lactic acid bacteria starter (DuPont) 0.02% | Same |

- Cured at 4 °C for 12 h → stuffed into pork casings (5 cm diameter, 20 cm long)
- **Fermentation:** 23 °C, 85–90% relative humidity, 48 h
- **Ripening:** 11–14 °C, 70% relative humidity, 21 days
- **Sampling:** day 0 (after stuffing), day 2 (after fermentation), days 9, 16, 23 (ripening days 7, 14, 21). Stored at −20 °C; triplicates

### Analyses (Sections 2.3–2.9)
| Group | Measured | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Proximate | Protein, fat, moisture, pH | Kjeldahl (factor 6.25), Soxhlet, drying at 105 °C, pH meter |
| Nitrite | NaNO₂ | Spectrophotometry at 538 nm |
| Precursors | Fatty acids / free amino acids | GC-FID (FAMEs) / automatic amino acid analyzer |
| Oxidation | TBARS (lipid) / carbonyls (protein) | 532 nm / DNPH method at 370 nm |
| Intermediates | 3-DG, GO, MGO | OPD derivatization, then LC-MS/MS (MRM) |
| AGEs | CML, CEL | Hexane defatting → NaBH₄ reduction → 6 M HCl hydrolysis at 110 °C for 24 h → MCX solid-phase extraction → LC-MS/MS (d₄ internal standards) |
| NAs | 9 volatile NAs | Acetonitrile extraction and clean-up → GC-MS/MS (EI, MRM, isotope-labeled internal standards) |

### Statistics (Section 2.10)
Mean ± SD of triplicates; SPSS 25; Kruskal-Wallis nonparametric test; Pearson correlation; PCA. Significance at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01.

## 4. Results (about 6 min)

### 4.1 Proximate composition and pH (Section 3.1, Fig. 1)
- Moisture fell during ripening, concentrating protein and fat.
- **More salt kept more moisture** (salt improves water-holding capacity).
- pH: with 2% salt it kept falling; with 0% and 4% it fell and then rose again. **The 4% salt samples had the highest pH**; the authors attribute this to excess salt inhibiting the starter bacteria, so less lactic acid was produced.
- Raw lean pork contained 74% moisture and backfat 14%. After 21 days, moisture no longer differed between fat ratios. Lean-only salami (10:0) had more protein and a higher pH during ripening.

### 4.2 Nitrite (Section 3.2, Fig. 2a–b)
- On day 0, nitrite was about 23 mg/kg, **only about 15% of the amount added**, showing it reacted quickly during curing.
- **It dropped sharply on days 0–2**, likely through conversion to nitrate, reactions with lipids and proteins, and nitrosamine formation.
- On day 2, salted samples retained more nitrite than unsalted ones (p < 0.05), which the authors relate to their higher pH.
- After day 16, nitrite rose again in the lean-only samples (interpreted as a reversible reaction at higher pH).

### 4.3 Precursors: free amino acids and fatty acids (Section 3.3, Fig. 2c–f, Fig. 3)
- Of **15 free amino acids**, histidine was the most abundant (34.4–61.5% of the total), followed by alanine, glutamic acid and lysine.
- Total free amino acids generally increased, but **the 4% salt samples showed no significant change** (lower water activity inhibits proteases and microbes).
- **In lean-only salami, they rose by 246% over 23 days.** Samples with added fat stayed relatively stable.
- Fatty acids were mostly monounsaturated (MUFA, 42.17–186.19 mg/g) and saturated (SFA, 40.72–160.63 mg/g). Oleic acid made up 92–93% of MUFA and linoleic acid 86.2–88.8% of PUFA.
- Salami with 40% fat had 1.43 times more SFA, 1.38 times more MUFA and 1.76 times more PUFA than salami with 20% fat.

### 4.4 Lipid and protein oxidation (Section 3.4, Fig. 4a–d)
- **TBARS (lipid oxidation):** rose with 0% and 2% salt but **fell in the later stage with 4% salt**. This fits earlier reports that salt is pro-oxidant at low concentrations and antioxidant above 2% (citing Rhee et al., 1983).
- With added fat, TBARS rose sharply (days 2–9); **lean-only salami stayed near zero**. The decline after day 9 is attributed to MDA reacting with proteins.
- **Carbonyls (protein oxidation):** increased throughout ripening. **Salt had no effect**, and 20% fat gave the highest values. The authors suggest lipid oxidation products bind proteins and worsen protein oxidation.

### 4.5 α-Dicarbonyls (Section 3.5, Fig. 5)
| Compound | Range (mg/kg, days 0–23) | Trend |
|---|---|---|
| 3-DG | 0.09–0.39 | Decreased |
| GO | 0.47–1.58 | Increased |
| MGO | 0.26–3.00 | Increased |

- **3-DG decreased:** it comes mainly from carbohydrates, and only 1% glucose was added under low-temperature processing, so its precursor ran short.
- **GO and MGO increased:** they form through several routes, including sugar oxidation, the Maillard reaction and lipid oxidation.
- **Salt reduced 3-DG and MGO** (no difference between 2% and 4%), which the authors attribute to salt inhibiting proteolysis and fermentative bacteria and so limiting Maillard precursors.
- Salami with 40% fat initially had more GO (lipid oxidation). After day 9, GO increased markedly in the lean-only samples (protein breakdown and more available lysine).

### 4.6 AGEs: CML and CEL (Section 3.6, Fig. 4e–h) ★ key result
- Over 23 days, **CML ranged 1.00–6.72 mg/kg and CEL 1.14–22.55 mg/kg**; both increased.
- **Salt:**
  - During fermentation (days 0–2), salt promoted lipid oxidation and increased CML.
  - During ripening (days 9–16), salt had little effect on CML.
  - **On day 23, 2% and 4% salt reduced CEL by 66% compared with no salt**, attributed to suppression of its precursor MGO and of protein and lipid breakdown.
- **Fat:** salami with added fat had **significantly more CML but less CEL** (lipid oxidation → GO → CML). CEL was highest in lean-only salami, which contained the most protein and lysine.
- **Meaning:** CML and CEL form through different pathways.

### 4.7 N-nitrosamines (Section 3.7, Fig. 6)
- Of the 9 NAs analyzed, **4 were detected: NDMA, NDEA, NDBA and NDPhA**. NDMA was the most abundant, followed by NDPhA. NPIP and NPYR were not detected, likely because no spices such as pepper were used.
- **Salt:**
  - NDMA and NDEA peaked on day 16 and then declined.
  - On day 23, NDEA and NDBA were highest with 2% salt. The authors attribute this to the lower pH of the low-salt (0 and 2%) samples, which favors nitrosation. 4% salt reduced NDMA and NDEA by inhibiting microbial and enzymatic activity.
  - Salt inhibited NDBA until day 23. Salt had no significant effect on NDPhA.
- **Fat:**
  - All NAs **rose sharply between days 16 and 23**.
  - **Salami with 40% fat had the lowest NDMA, NDEA and NDBA.**
  - NDPhA was **highest in lean-only salami**, which contained the most protein and free amino acids.
  - Salami with 20% fat had the highest NDMA, NDEA and NDBA, which the authors link to its highest carbonyl (protein oxidation) levels.

### 4.8 Correlation analysis and PCA (Section 3.8, Figs. 7–8)
- **PCA:** salt experiment PC1 63.6% + PC2 12.7% = 76.3%; fat experiment PC1 48.8% + PC2 30.5% = 79.3%. Samples moved toward the hazardous compounds as ripening progressed.
- **Correlations:**
  - **CML:** correlated with fat, fatty acids, TBARS and carbonyls → lipid oxidation pathway
  - **CEL:** correlated with protein, free amino acids, carbonyls, MGO and GO → protein-related Maillard pathway
  - **The 4 NAs:** strongly correlated with each other (shared nitrosation pathway) and with protein and carbonyls → protein oxidation supplies precursors
  - **NDPhA:** positively correlated with free amino acids and negatively with fatty acids
  - CEL correlated with NDPhA, but CML did not correlate with NAs.

## 5. Conclusions (Section 4, about 1 min)

- AGEs and NAs kept increasing during fermentation and ripening, driven by the Maillard reaction, nitrosation and lipid peroxidation together.
- Salt improved water retention and **significantly inhibited 3-DG, MGO, CEL and NDBA**.
- Fat **increased CML but reduced GO, CEL and NDPhA**.
- The authors conclude that **2% salt with 40% fat was the best formulation for reducing AGEs**. For NAs, the effect of salt depended on the NA type, and the lowest NA levels were seen **only with 40% fat**.
- Controlling salt and fat may help reduce AGEs and NAs in low-temperature fermented meat products.

## 6. Critical appraisal (presenter's view, about 1 min)

These are the presenter's interpretations of the paper. Present them as "points to consider," not as conclusions.

1. **Same recipe, different results:** The 2% salt sample in the salt experiment and the 8:2 sample in the fat experiment have the same recipe (lean:fat 8:2, 2% salt). Yet day-23 NDMA reads approx. **4 mg/kg (Fig. 6a) vs approx. 25 mg/kg (Fig. 6e)** from the graphs. The authors themselves mention batch-to-batch variation to explain why the fat experiment showed no decline at day 23 (p.10).
2. **Nitrosamine units:** Results are reported in mg/kg, far higher than the non-volatile NAs in Danish retail cured meats cited in the introduction (3.1–1660 ng/g). The units and quantification may need checking.
3. **Trade-off with 40% fat:** AGEs and NAs fell, but TBARS (lipid oxidation) and fat intake rose, and CML actually increased.
4. **Statistics and interpretation:** n = 3 per treatment with a Kruskal-Wallis test gives low statistical power. Correlation analysis does not show causation.
5. **Range of conditions:** Only one nitrite level (0.015%) was tested, and changes after day 23 were not measured (the authors note a later decline cannot be ruled out).
6. **Figure error in the original:** The y-axis of Fig. 7B is labeled "PC1=30.5%", but the text identifies it as PC2.

## 8. Anticipated questions

- **If 40% fat is optimal, should we add more fat?** → Looking only at AGEs and NAs, yes, but lipid oxidation and fat intake increase and CML rises. Keep the answer within the scope of the authors' conclusion.
- **Is lower-salt salami healthier?** → In this study, salami with 0% salt had the highest CEL. Reducing salt may increase other hazardous compounds.
- **Why do CML and CEL behave differently?** → CML correlated with lipid oxidation markers, and CEL with protein and MGO. The interpretation is that their precursors (GO vs MGO) form through different routes.
- **Why did 3-DG decrease?** → It forms mainly from sugars, but only 1% glucose was added under low-temperature processing, so its precursor ran short.
